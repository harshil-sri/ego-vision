# Research gap map: when should an egocentric assistant speak?

Status: working document, 2026-11-11. It records what was read, what was
measured, and what is still a guess. Read-depth is stated for every paper,
because a gap claim is only as strong as the reading behind it.

## 1. The question

A head-mounted camera watches a person do a manual task. An assistant can
speak at any moment. Two separate questions decide a good moment:

1. **Need.** Does the person need help now? (A step ended, a mistake began,
   a hazard appeared.)
2. **Interruptibility.** Can the person take in speech now without losing
   time or making an error? (Hands in a fine-motor action, or free?)

The claim examined here: current work models question 1 and almost never
question 2 from the hands.

What the nearest works do and do not do:

- **ProMemAssist** has an explicit interruption cost, but it comes from a
  working-memory model. Its future-work section names hand interaction, gaze
  and head pose as cues it does not yet use to assess load.
- **Pro²Assist** uses hand cues, but to understand the step (need), not to
  decide whether the person can listen. A second passage read confirms
  the direction: optical flow in the hand regions selects "key moments",
  and frames with MORE hand motion are treated as more likely to need
  assistance. It stays silent only when its reasoner finds no need. In
  the pilot below, human instructors do the opposite for routine speech:
  they speak less while the hands are in fine-motor actions.
- **TRAFA** uses hand motion to predict an error early. That is also need.
  The passages read do not include an interruption-cost term.
- **EgoServe** scores timing against event windows. The passages read do not
  mention hand state or interruption cost. Its abstract does say that
  earlier systems ignore "whether assistance would actually be welcome",
  so the Sato lab already frames "whether to speak" as the problem. Their
  answer is memory and context, not the hands.
- **EgoVoice** is the closest to this pilot in data: it trains on the same
  HoloAssist instructor onsets. It scores onset timing with a window of
  2.5 s early to 1.5 s late, and it measures whether the utterance ends
  before the user's next action. The passages read contain no hand-state
  input, no interruption cost, and no treatment of mistakes that the
  instructor left alone. Its best model reaches onset recall 0.30, so the
  timing problem is far from solved.
- **Jarvis** shows that "stay silent when people correct themselves" is an
  existing idea, in spoken conversation. It has not been found here for
  physical tasks seen through an egocentric camera.

## 3. The gap, stated narrowly

No work found here does all three of these:

1. estimates interruptibility from the observed hand-object state,
2. weighs it against the urgency of the message, and
3. validates the result against the timing of real human instructors.

Limits of this claim:

- The search covered 2025 to 2026 arXiv work found by keyword search. It is
  not a systematic review. A first pass over HCI venues (CHI, UIST,
  UbiComp) was done on 2026-11-11 by keyword search, abstracts mostly.
  Interruptibility sensing is 20 years old there, for desk and phone
  work.
- Two neighbours found in that pass narrow item 1. Sensible Agent senses
  whether the hands are available and adapts the form of a suggestion to
  it; it does not time the suggestion and has no human timing data. The
  HoloAssist benchmark already shows that hand pose helps to predict the
  TYPE of the next intervention. So "hands as an input" is not new.
  What is not found: hand state as a COST term that delays or cancels
  speech, tested against when instructors actually speak and stay silent.
- "Whether to speak" and "the cost of interrupting" are not new ideas.
  Vinci2, ProMemAssist, Jarvis and the 2026 survey all state them. What
  is claimed here is narrower: nobody found so far grounds them in the
  observed hand state and in real instructor silence on physical tasks.
- Seven papers were read in passages, the rest in abstract only. A paper
  marked A may contain a hand-state term that the abstract does not mention.
- The field moves fast: an ECCV 2026 challenge track on proactive timing
  exists. A paper filling this gap could appear at any time.

Before any submission, do a full read of the seven P papers and a search of
the HCI literature on interruptibility sensing.

## 4. Pilot evidence (HoloAssist; parts 1 to 4 labels only, part 5 video)

Data: 1,568 usable videos, 113.8 h, 22,827 instructor utterance onsets,
hand actions annotated by humans. Baseline for parts 1 and 3: each video's
onsets shifted by one random offset, which keeps their spacing and breaks
only their alignment with the hands.

### Part 1: where onsets fall (`reports/holoassist_timing.md`, 2,000 shifts)

Ratio = observed share of onsets in a hand state divided by the chance share.

| Hand state at onset | Follow-up | High-level | Confirmation | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| idle | 1.16 | 2.08 | 1.30 | 0.65 |
| hold | 1.83 | 1.45 | 1.06 (n.s.) | 1.47 |
| inspect | 2.05 | 0.54 | 0.69 | 1.24 |
| insert | 0.50 | 0.20 | 0.53 | 1.09 (n.s.) |
| unscrew | 0.40 | 0.11 | 0.17 | 0.63 |
| screw | 0.23 | 0.15 | 0.37 | 0.26 |

- Routine speech avoids fine-motor actions. All three routine types fall
  during screwing at 0.15 to 0.37 times the chance rate.
- Corrections do not avoid busy hands in general. They are less frequent
  than chance in idle moments (0.65) and more frequent during assemble,
  press, pull, rotate and align (1.5 to 2.2).
- 63% of matched corrections start before the wrong action ends. The median
  correction starts 2.0 s after the wrong action starts.

### Part 2: prediction (`reports/holoassist_predict.md`)

Task: at each 0.5 s tick, predict a routine instructor onset in the next
0.5 s. Base rate 2.25%. 5 folds, split by video.

| Features | ROC AUC | Average precision |
|---|---|---|
| Task progress only | 0.769 | 0.068 |
| Hand state only | 0.737 | 0.053 |
| Task progress + hand state | 0.805 | 0.087 |

Hand state adds information beyond task-progress features. The hand state
is the human annotation, so this is an upper bound for a recogniser.

### Part 2b: the hand-state effect, adjusted for need (`reports/holoassist_adjusted.md`)

Logistic regression on the same ticks. Odds of a routine onset in the next
0.5 s, relative to idle hands, at equal values of the four need proxies
and with each video's own onset rate as a covariate (so quiet and
talkative sessions are not compared with each other). 95% intervals from
40 refits on resampled videos.

| Hand state | Odds ratio against idle | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| unscrew | 0.17 | 0.15 to 0.24 |
| screw | 0.19 | 0.17 to 0.23 |
| insert | 0.32 | 0.29 to 0.35 |
| grab | 0.47 | 0.45 to 0.50 |
| align, withdraw, pull, lift, press | 0.53 to 0.56 | |
| rotate | 0.69 | 0.65 to 0.76 |
| inspect | 0.83 | 0.80 to 0.92 |
| place | 0.94 | 0.85 to 0.99 |
| hold | 0.94 | 0.89 to 1.17 |

- The states form a scale. Tool and fine-motor actions are at the bottom;
  holding, placing and inspecting are close to idle hands.
- The between-video term matters. Without it the screw odds ratio is 0.13,
  with it 0.19: sessions with long screwing also have quieter instructors.
- The need proxies are crude, so "adjusted" is limited to those four
  numbers. This table is the candidate "interruptibility by hand state"
  prior for a model; it is not yet evidence of a cost.

### Part 3: the hands around the utterance (`reports/holoassist_aftermath.md`, 500 shifts)

Extra idle time in the 5 s after the onset, compared with chance:

| Utterance type | All onsets | Hands busy at onset |
|---|---|---|
| Follow-up instruction | +0.09 s | +0.01 s (n.s.) |
| High-level instruction | +0.98 s | +0.48 s |
| Confirmation | +0.80 s | +0.62 s |
| Correction | -0.03 s (n.s.) | +0.22 s (same before: +0.21 s) |

- Performers keep working through follow-up instructions.
- Around high-level instructions the hands stop for about 1 s more than
  chance. The drop starts about 2 s before the onset and is deepest just
  after it (see `reports/figures/holoassist_aftermath.png`).

### Part 4: mistakes the instructor leaves alone (`reports/holoassist_silence.md`)

Of 7,113 wrong actions, the instructor corrected 57% and the performer
fixed 43% alone. Task: predict "instructor stays silent" from what is
known when the mistake starts. 5 folds, split by recording session.

| Features | ROC AUC, all mistakes | ROC AUC, "drop" removed |
|---|---|---|
| Verb of the wrong action | 0.790 | 0.666 |
| Verb + object + task type | 0.861 | 0.779 |
| History (time in task, time since instructor spoke, mistakes and corrections so far) | 0.789 | 0.783 |
| All | 0.896 | 0.832 |

- "drop" is 16% of wrong actions and 99% self-corrected: a dropped part is
  obvious to the performer. It inflates the first column, so the second
  column is the fair one.
- Silence is not random. It depends on the kind of mistake, on the task
  (15% silent for "assemble utility cart", 89% for "disassemble
  nightstand") and on the recent history of the session.
- Limit: a quick self-correction leaves the instructor no time to speak.
  Some "silence" is therefore lack of opportunity, not a decision. The
  duration of the wrong action cannot be used to check this, because a
  correction itself cuts the action short.

### Part 5: the same questions from video (`reports/holoassist_from_video.md`)

Parts 1 to 4 read the hand state from human labels. Part 5 uses the
camera: 305 videos from 64 recording sessions (a random 20% of the usable
videos, whole sessions, 22 GB), one frozen VideoMAE vector per second
(the verb model's backbone, never trained on HoloAssist). Ticks are 1 s
here (parts 2 and 2b: 0.5 s), so the numbers are comparable inside this
table only. Splits are by session.

How well does a small classifier on the video vector name the labelled
hand state? 22 states (20 verbs, other verb, idle): accuracy 0.36
(always the most common state: 0.18). Idle against busy: ROC AUC 0.79.
Best states: screw 0.57, unscrew 0.49, insert 0.42.

Predicting a routine instructor onset in the next second (base rate
4.36%):

| Features | ROC AUC | Average precision |
|---|---|---|
| Task progress only | 0.756 | 0.109 |
| Task progress + labelled hand state | 0.792 | 0.134 |
| Task progress + hand state predicted from video | 0.789 | 0.132 |
| Task progress + raw video vector (32 components) | 0.778 | 0.124 |

- The predicted hand state recovers about 90% of the gain that the human
  labels give. Gain in average precision over task progress, with 95%
  intervals from 1,000 resamples of recording sessions: labelled +0.024
  (0.018 to 0.031), predicted +0.022 (0.016 to 0.029). Predicted minus
  labelled: -0.001 (-0.009 to +0.007), so the two are not distinguishable
  here.
- This holds although the classifier is right only 36% of the time on
  22 states. The reason, checked in `reports/holoassist_shift_control.md`:
  on the three groups that matter for timing (fine-motor: screw, unscrew,
  insert; free: idle, hold, place, inspect; other) it is right 64% of
  the time, it finds 75% of the fine-motor ticks, and it calls a
  fine-motor tick "free" only 3% of the time. Its weak point is the
  free group (35% found; most are called "other").
- Routing the video through the hand-state classifier works better than
  giving the timing model the video vector directly: +0.008 (0.000 to
  0.015), a small margin.
- The same data as a shift test (`reports/holoassist_shift_control.md`):
  routine onsets fall in ticks with a PREDICTED fine-motor state (screw,
  unscrew, insert; 31% of the time) at 0.35 times the chance rate, and in
  predicted free-hand states (idle, hold, place, inspect) at 1.22 times.
  With the human labels: 0.38 and 1.32. This is the control for the
  Ego-EXTRA test in section 8.

Mistakes the instructor leaves alone (1,316 mistakes in the subset, 1,115
without "drop"; ROC AUC):

| Features | All mistakes | "drop" removed |
|---|---|---|
| History | 0.712 | 0.710 |
| Video (window ending about 1 s after the mistake starts) | 0.667 | 0.606 |
| History + video | 0.741 | 0.730 |
| Labels + history | 0.849 | 0.738 |

- Video adds little to history here (0.02 to 0.03). With "drop" removed,
  history + video is close to labels + history. The subset is small for
  this question, and no intervals were computed.

- In the app: the classifier is available as an experimental signal
  (`src/pipeline/hand_state.py`). On EPIC-KITCHENS ticks it gives a mean
  fine-motor probability of 0.20 for wash, 0.18 for cut, 0.13 for mix,
  and 0.06 to 0.07 for take, put, open, close and no-action ticks. That
  ordering is plausible; it is not a validation (EPIC has no hand-state
  labels, and "fine-motor" was defined on screws, not knives).

- A result that weakens the cost reading
  (`reports/holoassist_shift_control.md`, "by type of instructor
  utterance"). With the three groups, CORRECTIONS also avoid fine-motor
  ticks: 0.41 times chance with predicted state, 0.51 with labels
  (follow-up 0.35, high-level 0.18, confirmation 0.45). The simple
  explanation is need, not politeness: over all HoloAssist labels,
  fine-motor actions take 40% of the action time and hold 19% of the
  instructor-corrected mistakes (0.5 mistakes per minute, against 1.9 for
  "other" actions). People who are screwing rarely do something wrong.
  The part 1 contrast survives only in the free group: high-level
  instructions are 1.67 times chance there, corrections 0.82 to 1.00.
  So "instructors avoid fine-motor moments" is mostly explained by
  "nothing needs saying then". A cost claim needs the controlled study
  (section 6, item 3) or a design that holds need fixed.

### Part 6: hand state at fixed need (`reports/holoassist_need_controlled.md`)

Labels only, 1,568 videos. Two situations where something does need
saying. Odds ratios from a per-tick logistic model with time since the
start of the episode as a spline; intervals from 100 refits on resampled
videos.

Test A, a next-step instruction is pending (3,928 episodes: a coarse step
boundary, then a high-level instruction within 20 s). Odds that the
instruction starts in the next 0.5 s, against idle hands, at equal time
since the boundary:

| Hand state | Odds ratio | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| Fine-motor action (screw, unscrew, insert) | 0.17 | 0.13 to 0.23 |
| Other action | 0.30 | 0.28 to 0.33 |
| Hold, place, inspect | 0.63 | 0.56 to 0.72 |

Test B, a mistake is in progress and the instructor will correct it
(3,256 mistakes, 639 of them in fine-motor actions). Odds that the
correction starts in the next 0.5 s, at equal time since the mistake
started:

| Term | Odds ratio | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong action still going on (not fine-motor) | 0.66 | 0.59 to 0.73 |
| Fine-motor x still going on | 0.99 | 0.84 to 1.20 |

- A: even with an instruction pending, it starts mostly when the hands
  are idle, and least during fine-motor work.
- B: corrections come somewhat later than "as soon as possible" for any
  action, and fine-motor work adds no extra delay.
- Together this is the shape the first idea predicted: a low-urgency
  message waits for the hands, an urgent one does not. It is the best
  support so far, and it is from the full dataset.
- From video (`reports/holoassist_need_controlled_video.md`, 305 videos,
  1 s ticks, predicted group against the "free" group): test A gives
  0.22 (0.10 to 0.41) for predicted fine-motor ticks, and 0.24 (0.13 to
  0.39) with the labels on the same ticks. Test B gives 1.25 (0.85 to
  1.89): no sign that corrections wait. So the part 6 pattern does not
  depend on the human labels.
- The caveat for A stays: a performer who stops and waits after a step
  is idle BECAUSE the next instruction is needed. Idle hands then signal
  need, and the instructor may be reacting to that, not sparing busy
  hands. Test A cannot tell the two apart; test B is not affected.

### What the pilot does not show

- **Cause.** Instructors may speak in idle moments because the step just
  ended (need), not because the performer can listen (interruptibility).
  High-level instructions cluster at coarse step boundaries (3.8 times
  chance), so need and free hands arrive together. The pause after a
  high-level instruction can be "waiting for the next instruction" as much
  as "stopping to listen".
- **Cost.** Nothing here measures whether badly timed speech hurts the
  performer.
- **Vision.** Part 5 uses video on 20% of the videos, with a frozen
  backbone and offline features. It is not a live system, and the timing
  targets are still human speech, not a measured benefit.
- **Transfer.** HoloAssist is assembly and device tasks, not cooking.

The contrast between routine speech and corrections is the strongest item:
the same instructors, the same tasks, and the avoidance of busy hands
depends on the type of message. A "need" account can still explain part
of it: a person who is screwing usually knows what to do, so need is low
then. Part 2 controls for need only with crude task-progress proxies. The
contrast is evidence that fits the interruptibility idea, not proof of it.

## 5. Observation on the EgoServe benchmark

EgoServe (HF `SitongGong/EgoServe`) has 1,717 events on 191 HoloAssist
videos. Compared with the original human labels:

- `next_step_guidance` windows are the real instructor utterance intervals
  in 941 of 965 cases.
- `error_recovery`, `tool_use` and `safety` windows are the interval of the
  wrong action itself. In many of these the human instructor never spoke,
  because the performer fixed the mistake alone: error_recovery 258
  instructor-corrected against 144 self-corrected; tool_use 102 against 123;
  safety 1 against 12.

So about 280 of 748 error-type target windows ask a model to speak where
the human expert stayed silent. Across all of HoloAssist, 42.5% of wrong
actions were self-corrected without instructor speech. Whether silence was
the better choice in those cases is unknown, but a benchmark that rewards
speaking there measures something other than expert timing. This needs a
check against the EgoServe paper's own description of how windows were
built before it is stated publicly.

## 6. Candidate contributions, revised 2026-11-11

The first version of this section put "a gate that respects busy hands,
validated against instructor timing" on top. The evidence since then
(section 7, status) does not support "instructors hold back because the
hands are busy". The order is therefore changed.

1. **A "should stay silent" evaluation.** Unchanged, and now first. Use
   the mistakes that performers fix alone as negatives: a good assistant
   should not speak on them. Existing benchmarks do not score this
   (section 5). Part 4: learnable from labels (AUC 0.83 without "drop").
   Part 5: from history + video 0.73 on a small subset, no intervals.
   Next: the full HoloAssist videos, and Ego-EXTRA protocol 2 as a second
   source of "the expert chose silence".
2. **Need-controlled test of interruption cost, from existing data.**
   First version done (part 6): pending instructions wait for idle hands
   (odds 0.17 during fine-motor work), corrections do not wait more for
   fine-motor work (0.99). Open: separate "idle because waiting for the
   instruction" from "free to listen" in test A, for example with gaze
   (does the performer look at the next part or look around?), and repeat
   with video-predicted states. The original question was: Find pairs of moments with the same need (same
   step, same kind of pending instruction) and different hand state, and
   compare what the instructor does. HoloAssist has coarse step labels
   for this. If hand state still matters at equal need, the cost reading
   is back; if not, it is closed with evidence.
3. **Worker-side signal: when does the person want to talk?** Supported
   on both datasets: people start speaking less during fine-motor work,
   and gaze behaves differently before unasked and asked expert turns. An
   assistant could use this to predict questions and to time its turn in
   a dialogue. Smaller claim, better supported.
4. **A small controlled study of cost.** Same instruction delivered during
   fine-motor work or at the next free moment; task time and errors
   measured. Still the only route to a causal claim. Needs participants.

Items 1 and 2 can share one paper: a metric that benchmarks lack, plus a
careful negative or positive answer on hand-state cost.

## 7. What would weaken the idea

- A full read finds that Pro²Assist, EgoVoice or a 2026 paper already
  gates on hand state.
- With predicted (not annotated) hand state, the gain over task-progress
  features disappears.
- The routine-versus-correction contrast is explained by annotation
  practice (for example, annotators segmenting actions around speech).
  Check: repeat part 1 with action boundaries from the hand-pose stream
  instead of the labels.
- Status 2026-11-11, evening: three results now weaken the idea as first
  stated. (1) On HoloAssist, corrections avoid fine-motor moments as much
  as routine speech does, and mistakes are rare there: need explains most
  of the effect (section 4, part 5). (2) On Ego-EXTRA, measured hand
  motion has no relation to unasked expert speech. (3) On Ego-EXTRA, the
  video-based hand state trained on HoloAssist shows no avoidance by the
  expert either; it shows that the TRAINEE speaks less during fine-motor
  work (section 8, item 4). What is left standing: hand state from video
  predicts when dialogue happens on HoloAssist (part 5), and the person
  doing the task talks less when the hands are in fine work, on both
  datasets' closest scenarios. That is a statement about the worker, not
  about a cost that the helper respects.
  Counterweight, same evening (part 6): at fixed need on the full
  HoloAssist labels, a pending routine instruction waits for idle hands
  and a correction does not wait more for fine-motor work. That is the
  predicted shape, with one caveat that test A cannot remove (idle hands
  can themselves signal need). The idea is weakened as a claim about
  cost, not refuted as a description of when instructors speak.
- Status 2026-11-11, afternoon: the second item did not happen (part 5: the gain
  stays with predicted hand state). The third item is open and the first
  attempt at it does not support the idea: on Ego-EXTRA, measured wrist
  speed has no relation to when experts speak unasked (section 8, item
  4). Two readings are possible. Either the HoloAssist effect is about
  the TYPE of manipulation, which speed does not capture, or it is partly
  produced by how HoloAssist actions were labelled. The decisive check:
  a hand-state classifier (trained on HoloAssist labels, as in part 5)
  applied to the Ego-EXTRA videos (23.7 GB), or finger-level features
  from the 21 hand landmarks.

## 8. Next steps

1. Full read of the seven P papers, plus EgoVoice, and an HCI search.
2. Done on a 305-video subset (part 5), with intervals. Open: the
   full 1,493 usable videos (about 80 GB more).
3. Build the stay-silent metric from the self-corrected wrong actions.
4. Ego-EXTRA as a second dataset: it has gaze, hand keypoints and real
   expert speech, and it includes kitchen and bakery scenes (closer to this
   project than HoloAssist). Access checked 2026-11-11: direct downloads
   from the project page (fpv-iplab.github.io/Ego-EXTRA), no form;
   non-commercial use only. Sizes: transcripts 1 MB, gaze 0.8 GB, hand
   keypoints 2.2 GB, full trainee videos 23.7 GB, question clips 64 GB.
   First result, transcripts only (`reports/egoextra_speech.md`, 136
   videos, 52 h): experts who were told to speak only when asked or when
   necessary still start a median of 0.8 unasked turns per minute with at
   least 1.5 s of speech (0.5 to 1.2 across threshold choices). The
   proactive protocol gives 0.9. The protocol halves the expert's talking
   time (47% to 26%) and does not change how often the expert speaks up
   unasked. The transcripts are machine-translated from Italian; only the
   time stamps and speaker tags are used.
   Second result, a negative one (`reports/egoextra_hands.md`, 98 videos,
   38.5 h): with the hand state taken from the glasses' hand tracking
   (wrist speed over the last 1 s: still, mid, moving, or no hand in
   view), unasked expert speech is NOT timed around hand speed. Onsets in
   "still" moments: 0.94 times chance; in "moving" moments: 0.97 times
   chance; both inside or at the edge of the range of 1,000 random
   shifts. The one deviation: 1.27 times chance when no hand is in view
   (95% of shifts: 0.80 to 1.21). Hand speed around the onset is flat
   (0.98 to 1.04 times the video average from 4 s before to 4 s after).
   Limits of this check: (a) the time alignment of tracking and
   transcript is assumed, and 32 videos were left out because the
   durations differ; (b) the speed is in the frame of the glasses, so it
   contains head motion; (c) wrist speed is a poor stand-in for "busy":
   in HoloAssist the most avoided states were screw, unscrew and insert,
   where the wrist hardly travels.
   Follow-up for limits (b) and (c) (`reports/egoextra_hands_fingers.md`,
   82 videos with finger landmarks): finger motion = change of the hand's
   shape, which head motion cannot produce. Same result: unasked long
   expert turns in "still" 1.01, "moving" 0.98 times chance. Check on
   limit (a): TRAINEE speech does relate to both signals (fewer turn
   starts when the hands are still, 0.86 to 0.88 times chance, outside
   the range of the shifts), so the alignment is not grossly wrong.
   Gaze (`reports/egoextra_hands_gaze.md`, 90 videos; same test with the
   trainee's eye tracking): the first measured signal with a relation.
   Unasked expert turns start less often while the eyes jump around
   (highest third of gaze speed): long turns 0.91 times chance (95% of
   shifts: 0.94 to 1.07), short turns ("ok", "perfect") 0.82 (0.90 to
   1.09). Gaze speed is 9% below the video average 4 s before an unasked
   long turn and 11% above it 4 s after. Expert turns that follow trainee
   speech show the opposite (eyes jumping: 1.14; eyes resting: 0.80).
   One reading: the expert speaks up when the trainee's eyes have settled,
   and the trainee then looks around; when the trainee is searching, the
   trainee asks first. The effect is small, it is one of four signals
   tested, and gaze distance (`_gaze_depth.md`) shows nothing for long
   turns. Treat it as a lead, not a result.
   By scenario (01 bike workshop, 02 bakery, 03 furniture assembly, 04
   kitchen; same reports): the gaze effect for long turns is not stable
   (eyes jumping: 0.73 in the bike workshop and the bakery, 1.01 in
   assembly, 0.94 in the kitchen). For short turns it has the same sign everywhere (0.75 to
   0.88). In assembly, the scenario closest to HoloAssist, unasked long
   turns are RARER when the wrist is still (0.82; 95% of shifts 0.89 to
   1.10). That fits limit (c): in assembly a still wrist often means
   fine work, not free hands. The video-based test below addresses this.

   The HoloAssist pattern does NOT transfer to unasked expert speech.
   Unasked turns are slightly MORE frequent in predicted fine-motor
   moments, also in furniture assembly (0.95, inside the range). What
   does transfer is the trainee's side: trainees start to speak less
   during predicted fine-motor moments (0.90 overall, 0.77 in assembly),
   and the expert's answers follow (0.80 overall, 0.61 in assembly, 0.59
   in the kitchen).
   Is the classifier meaningful on this video? Two label-free signs say
   partly yes: it predicts "fine" for 34% of the ticks with a still wrist
   and 13% with a moving wrist, and "free" for 21% of the ticks with no
   tracked hand against 10% to 14% otherwise. And it finds structure in
   trainee speech. It was still never checked against labels here.
